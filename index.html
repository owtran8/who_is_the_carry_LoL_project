01 / The question

What does it mean to “carry” a game?

League of Legends is a 5 vs. 5 game. Each player fills one role—top, jungle, mid, bot, or support—and contributes to a win differently. But can we compare their impact fairly?

Research question: Among winning professional League of Legends teams, which role most often has the highest impact, after accounting for the different jobs of each role?

I use “main impact player” instead of just “carry.” A bot laner may stand out through damage, while a support may excel through assists and vision. The goal is to compare players to others who do the same job, not reward damage alone.

02 / Data and method

From match records to a role-adjusted score

The source is the 2026 Oracle's Elixir professional match extract, covering January 1–November 15, 2026. Each game can have ten player records (five per side) and two team-summary records. I excluded team-summary rows and kept complete five-player winning teams.

9,932Winning teams analyzed
49,660Winning player rows
5Roles per team

Five impact signals

  • KDA: kills and assists relative to deaths
  • Kill participation: share of the team's kills a player helped secure
  • Damage per minute: offensive output adjusted for game length
  • Vision per minute: contribution to revealing the map
  • Low death share: avoiding a disproportionate share of team deaths

Why within-role percentiles?

Instead of comparing a support's raw damage to a bot laner's damage, I rank each player against other players in the same role for every metric.

Impact score = mean(within-role metric percentiles)

On each winning five-player team, the player with the highest composite score receives main_impact = 1; the other four receive 0.

Important: This is a transparent, custom post-match scoring rule—not an official MVP award or a measure of who caused the victory.

Missing data matters

The source has incomplete timeline fields for some games. I checked how missingness varied with match-data completeness rather than assuming that all values were available. Missingness can affect which player contributions are measurable.

03 / Findings

Which role earned the main-impact label most often?

Mid ranked first at 21.30% of winning teams, followed closely by top, bot, and jungle. Support was lowest at 18.10%. The differences are small in magnitude, even when statistically detectable.

Are the role shares exactly equal?

I used a randomization test with Total Variation Distance (TVD) to compare the observed distribution with an idealized 20% per role.

Observed TVD

0.0190

A small overall departure from an equal 20/20/20/20/20 split.

Randomization p-value

0.0010

Under the simulated equal-role null, a departure this large was unusual.

Takeaway: Mid edged out the other roles under this scoring formula, but 21.30% does not prove mid lane is objectively the most valuable role. The scoring formula is deliberately role-normalized.

04 / Machine learning

Can a model identify the top-scoring player?

A binary logistic regression model predicts whether a player's completed-game performance matches the custom main-impact label. Every winning team contributes one positive example and four negative examples, so only 20% of players have the positive label.

Expanded model

Adds more engineered context to represent player contributions. Hyperparameter tuning is performed within earlier games, and evaluation is performed on later unseen games.

Engineered features

Matches were split chronologically by game, keeping players from the same match together. Training used earlier games (January–August 2026); the held-out test covered later games (August–November 2026).

What is F1?

F1 balances precision (how often predicted impact players were truly labeled as such) and recall (how many labeled impact players the model found). It is useful when only one of five players has the positive label.

What is top-1?

For each five-player team, rank the model's predicted probabilities and select its highest-scoring player. The expanded model matched the custom label for 68.5% of held-out teams.

05 / Interpretation

What these results do—and do not—show

What this project demonstrates

  • A reproducible player-level data cleaning pipeline
  • Within-role feature engineering and an interpretable impact score
  • A randomization test for role-balance differences
  • Chronological, grouped evaluation of two classification models
  • Clear communication of model trade-offs with charts and a confusion matrix

Limitations worth stating

  • The “main-impact” label is constructed from match statistics; it is not an external ground truth.
  • Classifying post-game statistics is not predicting future match winners.
  • Role normalization and chosen feature weights influence the outcome.
  • Repeated players/teams and missing timeline values complicate uncertainty estimates.
  • Correlation and classification do not prove causal contribution to a win.
Overall conclusion: The role-adjusted rankings are fairly balanced, with mid slightly ahead. Adding engineered player context improves post-match classification of the custom standout-player label on unseen later matches.
